# A visit, from the door to the plan

Primary care is the general visit. You bring a reason. Someone trained to see patients hears it, looks at the medicines, and decides what belongs in the room today and what belongs somewhere else. The rest of this page is that hour, in the order it usually happens.

## Why you came

The reason can be new. A cough that has outlasted the explanation you gave yourself. A headache pattern. Fatigue you have started to treat as personality. It can also be old: diabetes you already know about, blood pressure you have been writing down, a medicine you are not sure you are taking correctly.

Say the reason in ordinary words. You do not need a diagnosis to walk in with. If the problem is chest pain, trouble breathing, a sudden weakness, trouble speaking, severe bleeding, or fainting, do not save it for this visit. Call 911.

## The part with the cuff and the list

Blood pressure, and sometimes pulse, weight, or temperature, shows up when it would change the plan. Bring the bottles or a list, including pills you buy without a prescription. People forget the pain reliever they take every night and then cannot understand why the pressure or the stomach has changed.

A focused exam follows the reason you came. It is not a tour of every system on a checklist. Prevention gets attached to the same visit when it fits your age and history: a conversation about diabetes risk, cholesterol, smoking, cancer screening that is due, mood. National screening recommendations are a starting point. Your history decides which of them apply. A webpage list does not.

## Tests, medicines, and the next office

Basic labs happen when a clinician orders them. Ask where the blood is drawn and how you will hear the result. A lot of tests are not done in the exam room. That is ordinary. What matters is that you know the place and the date you should hear something.

Medicines are reviewed, refilled, or explained. If a dose is confusing, say so before you leave. If a specialist, a hospital, or an urgent care already started something, bring that paper. Primary care is often the place those pieces get put in one chart.

When the right next step is another office, you should leave with the name of it. A referral is part of the job. So is telling you when the problem can be handled here.

## What you should be able to repeat

Treatment, a test, a follow-up, or nothing further. One sentence. If the visit was in a language you are still learning, ask to say the plan back. ## A visit that does not finish everything

Some days the plan is “this part here, that part at another office.” That is a complete visit if you leave with the name of the other office and a way to reach this one when you get back. Primary care is often the coordination, not the procedure. If you expected an X-ray, a procedure, or a specialist in the same suite and that is not what this clinic does, you should hear that before you have rearranged your week around the wrong expectation. Ask on the phone: “I need a checkup” or “I need someone to look at my pressure” is a clearer request than “I need everything checked.”

Refills are the same idea. A medicine you have been taking for years still needs a person willing to keep prescribing it, which means they need to know you. Bring the bottle. If a specialist started it, bring the name of that specialist. A refill is not automatic just because the bottle is empty.

The clearest sign the visit worked is ordinary. You can name the next step. You know whether to come back, call, or wait for a result. You are not holding the whole story by yourself until the next crisis.
