# Screening is a short list, matched to you

The useful version of prevention is boring. A pressure. A question about diabetes if the risk is there. A cholesterol panel when it would change a plan. A cancer screening on the interval that fits your age and your last result. The less useful version is a long menu that treats every adult as the same body.

Recommendations move. The place clinicians check them is the [U.S. Preventive Services Task Force](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation-topics). What follows is the shape of the conversation, not a personal order.

## In your twenties and thirties

Blood pressure belongs in almost every adult visit. It is quiet, and a single sloppy reading is easy to misread, which is why the way it is taken matters as much as the number. Diabetes screening is not automatic at 22. It becomes reasonable earlier when weight, family history, a history of diabetes in pregnancy, or other risks are in the chart. Cholesterol is similar. Ask if you have never had one and heart disease showed up early in a parent.

Cervical screening, for people with a cervix, has its own clock. Colon and breast screening are usually later, unless your family history pulls them forward. If a relative was diagnosed young, say the age. “Cancer runs in the family” is too vague to change a date.

## In your forties and fifties

This is when several clocks overlap, which is why the visit feels crowded. Breast cancer screening for average-risk women is mammography, with the start and the interval depending on the guideline you and the clinician are using. Colon screening for average-risk adults now often starts at 45. Blood pressure, diabetes risk, and cholesterol are no longer theoretical.

Write down what you have already done. A mammogram in another city counts. A stool test from a health fair might count, if someone can tell you what kind it was. Repeating a test because the last result is in a portal you cannot open is common. Naming the office that has it saves a needle.

## After that

The question shifts from “what should I start” to “what should I stop, and what needs a closer interval.” Stopping a screening is a decision. It depends on age, other illnesses, and whether finding something would change what you would do. That is a harder conversation than adding a test, and it is a legitimate one.

## After an abnormal result

A screening test that comes back unusual is not the last word. It is a reason for a repeat, a different test, or a referral. Ask who calls, by when, and what silence means. Build the follow-up on the day the test is ordered. People lose results in the gap between “we’ll let you know” and a new job, a new phone, a new address.

If you want this sorted with a person instead of a list, that is an ordinary primary care visit. Bring the last results you have, even if they are incomplete.

## Blood pressure, diabetes risk, cholesterol

These three get bundled in conversation and they are still different tests. Blood pressure is a measurement in the room, or a log you kept at home. Diabetes risk may lead to an A1C or a fasting glucose, which are not the meter on your counter. Cholesterol is a panel. Sometimes you will be asked to fast, sometimes not. Ask before the morning of the draw so you do not fast for a test that did not require it, or eat before one that did.

A result in the past that was called “borderline” deserves the old paper. Borderline is not a diagnosis and it is not a clearance. It is a number that needed a date for the next look. If you never got that date, this visit is a reasonable time to ask for it.

## Cancer screening is not one package

Breast, cervical, colon, and lung screening apply to different people. Lung screening, where it is recommended at all, is tied to a heavy smoking history and an age range. It is not added because you once smoked at parties. Cervical screening depends on anatomy and prior results. Colon screening has more than one method, and the method changes what a “positive” means. A positive stool test is a reason for a colonoscopy, not a finished answer.

Bring the year of the last test of each kind, even if the result was “normal” and you threw the letter away. The year is what sets the next one.

## A list you can keep on a phone note

Three lines are enough. What was done, and the year. What was ordered today, and where. Who calls if the result is unexpected, and when you will call them if they do not. Update it after the visit, not from memory a month later. That note is more useful than a folder of reminder emails from four different portals.
