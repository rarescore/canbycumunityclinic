# The pharmacy machine beeped high

You stepped off the street, sat down, and the cuff squeezed. The number was higher than you expected. That can be hypertension. It can also be the last block you walked, a cuff that barely closed, a conversation with the person next to you, coffee, a full bladder, or the fact that you have never liked those machines.

One reading is a reason to measure properly. It is not, by itself, a treatment plan.

## Measure it like you mean it

Sit for about five minutes before you start. Use an upper-arm cuff that fits. The [American Heart Association’s measuring tips](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/understanding-blood-pressure-readings) are the short version clinicians actually want: back supported, feet on the floor, legs uncrossed, arm at heart level, no talking. Skip exercise, nicotine, and caffeine for about half an hour if you can.

Take two readings, a minute apart. Write both down, with the time. If someone asked you to track it, repeat that on several days rather than chasing the one dramatic number. Bring the cuff in once. A nurse can see whether the size looks right and how it compares with the office machine.

Wrist cuffs and the machines at the pharmacy are easy to reach. They are also easy to do badly. If that is the only number you have, say where it came from.

## What the number is for

Organizations do not all use the same cutoff, and a target depends on age, diabetes, kidney disease, pregnancy, and the medicines you already take. An internet chart is a poor way to start, stop, or double a blood-pressure pill. If the readings you are writing down stay above the range a clinician already gave you, call. If you are on medicine and the numbers jump, bring the bottles and mention anything new: cold medicine, pain relievers, a missed dose, a week of bad sleep.

## When you should not sit at home with it

A high number plus chest pain, trouble breathing, sudden weakness, trouble speaking, fainting, or a sudden severe change in thinking is an emergency. Call 911. Do not drive to a routine appointment to see if the number comes down.


## Why the office number and the home number disagree

People get told they have “white coat” readings, or the opposite, and then nobody writes down which was which. If you are relaxed at the kitchen table and high in the office, or fine in the office and high at home, both patterns matter. The way to show them is a short log: date, time, two numbers, and a note if you had just climbed stairs or taken a pain reliever.

Do not average a month of readings in your head. Bring the paper or a photo of the paper. A clinician can say whether the pattern needs a medicine change, a bigger cuff, or nothing but a repeat in a few weeks.

If you already have a target written on an old after-visit summary, use that target. If you do not, do not invent one from a chart on a poster in the pharmacy. Ask for the range that applies to you, and ask what number should make you call before the next visit.

If you feel well and the only news is one high reading, the next step is a clean measurement and a person who can look at the pattern with you. That can be part of an ordinary weekday visit. Bring the paper.

If you are already on medicine, do not stop it because one home reading looked better than usual. Call and ask. A few calm days are not the same as a new plan. And if you have never been treated and the log is high on most days, that log is the thing to bring. The story of the single worst reading is the one people tell. The pattern is the one that gets used.
