# The gap is usually longer than you say out loud

People round down. “A couple of years” was four. “I go when I need to” means the last visit was an urgent care for a sinus infection, and nobody has taken a history since a job physical. You are not late in a moral sense. The chart is just empty, and the first visit has to build a floor under it.

Start with the reason you picked up the phone. Not with an apology. “I have not been seen since 2019 and my pressure at the pharmacy was 150” is a better opening than a speech about why you stayed away. The reasons are usually ordinary. Work. A ride. A bad visit somewhere else. Insurance that lapsed. A stretch of feeling fine that turned into a habit.

## What the first visit is trying to learn

Someone needs a current list of medicines, including the ones you buy yourself and the ones you were prescribed and stopped. Allergies, with the reaction. The illnesses you actually remember, not a complete autobiography. Whether anyone in the family had heart disease, diabetes, or cancer young enough that it changes your screening. Smoking, alcohol, and the kind of work you do, if it beats up your sleep or your back.

Blood pressure will almost certainly be part of it. Weight might. A lab might, if a clinician wants a baseline and you are willing to do the blood draw where it is actually done. You will not finish every test that exists. If someone offers you a full-body scan as a substitute for this visit, that is a different product. It is not the standard way back into care.

Ask which screening fits your age and which one can wait. Colon screening, breast screening, cervical screening, diabetes risk, cholesterol: each has a clock. A long gap is a reason to look at the clocks. It is not a reason to order all of them on the same Tuesday.

## The papers in the drawer

Find what you can without turning it into a project that delays the visit another year. A hospital discharge. A lab from the last place that drew your blood. The name of a pharmacy. A blood pressure notebook, even if it is three pages. If the rest is gone, say so. Clinics are used to rebuilding a history from memory.

Do not email those papers to a general inbox. Call and ask how the office wants them. A website form that asks for your name and phone is not a chart.

## The second visit is the one that counts

The first appointment restarts the file. The value shows up when something comes back and someone is still there to read it. A lab. A repeat pressure. A mammogram or a stool test scheduled before you talk yourself out of it. Put the next date on the calendar before the motivation fades. If the plan was “we’ll call you,” ask when you should call them if you have not heard.

Shame is part of this for a lot of people, and it keeps the gap open longer than any single symptom. You can say how long it has been in the words you have. If you only remember “sugar,” “pressure,” or “the medicine from the pharmacy on Sherman Way,” that is a start. The person in the room has heard a longer gap than yours.

## A month later

Look at what you were asked to do and what you actually did. Filled the medicine. Checked a pressure at home twice. Missed the lab because it was across town on a workday. The missed piece is the thing to say at the next visit, not a reason to skip it. Care after a long gap is two or three completed steps. One heroic visit that never gets a follow-up puts you back where you were, with a newer piece of paper in the drawer.

## What to do the week before

Pick the two facts you are most tempted to skip, and put them on the list anyway. The medicine you stopped. The amount you drink. The symptom that only happens at night. Clinicians cannot account for a fact they do not have, and the visit is shorter when those are said in the first five minutes instead of the last one.

If you need a ride, arrange it before you request the time. A visit you cannot get to is how another year goes by. If the person coming is your parent, ask them what they want said when they are in the room, and what they want said only if they bring it up. Adult children often narrate the whole history and the parent never gets a sentence in.

Wear something you can push a sleeve up on. Eat unless you were told to fast. Take your usual morning medicines unless you were told to hold one, and be ready to name which ones you took. Bring a pen. The plan you will be given is easier to follow if you write it in your own handwriting before you reach the parking lot.

If the first available conversation is by phone, use it. You can say how long it has been, what you are worried about in a sentence, and whether you need English or Spanish. You still should not send a medical history through a website form. The form is how the clinic gets a number to call. The history happens after a person picks up.
