# There is no single list that fits every woman

Age, anatomy, pregnancy, family history, and the last result all change the list. A visit is where that gets sorted. A poster in a waiting room is not.

## Heart risk is not a separate subject

Blood pressure, diabetes risk, cholesterol, and smoking sit in the same conversation as everything else. Heart disease is a leading cause of death for women, and it is still easy to spend the whole visit on one organ and never mention the cuff. If it has been years since anyone took your pressure, start there. It takes minutes.

## Breast screening

For women at average risk, the [U.S. Preventive Services Task Force](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/breast-cancer-screening) recommends mammography every two years from age 40 through 74. Other groups use different start ages and different intervals. A strong family history, a prior biopsy, or a gene change can move the plan earlier and can add MRI. That is a conversation, not a birthday rule you apply alone.

A lump, bloody nipple discharge, or a skin change is not a screening question. Do not wait for the next mammogram date to mention it. Call and say there is a symptom.

## Cervical screening

The interval depends on your age, whether you still have a cervix, and what the last test showed. Cytology and HPV testing are not interchangeable just because both are “the Pap.” Bring the year and the result if you have it. If the last test was abnormal, the next step may be a repeat or a referral, not a routine reminder on a postcard.

Pain, bleeding after sex, or bleeding after menopause is a symptom. It is not something to file under the next screening year.

## Colon screening, bone, and everything filed as “later”

Colon screening is for women too. Average-risk adults are generally offered it starting at 45, with the method and the end age decided individually. Stool tests and colonoscopy are different tools. A positive stool test still needs a colonoscopy. It is not a finished answer.

Bone-density testing comes up with age and with fracture risk, earlier when the risk is higher. Mention steroids you took for a long time, a fracture from a minor fall, or a parent who broke a hip.

Pregnancy plans, bleeding, contraception, menopause, and infections belong in the same visit if you want them there. Say if you want that part of the conversation in Spanish, or without a family member in the room.

You should leave knowing which test, if any, was ordered, where it happens, and who calls you. Canby Community Clinic can start that conversation in Reseda. Mammograms and procedures that are not done here are arranged at another office.

## What to bring if you have changed clinics

The year of your last mammogram, Pap or HPV test, and colon test. The result, if you have it. The name of the office that did it. A list of medicines, including hormones. Any biopsy or abnormal result you remember, with the year even if the details are fuzzy.

If you are pregnant or you might be, say so before any imaging is discussed. If you are past menopause and bleeding, say that at the start of the visit. It changes the order of the conversation.

You do not need to arrive with a printed guideline. You need the history the guideline would be applied to. The clinician’s job is to match it. Your job is to leave knowing what was ordered, what was deliberately not ordered, and when you should hear something.
